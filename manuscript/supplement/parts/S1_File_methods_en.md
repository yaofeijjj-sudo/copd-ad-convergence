# Supplementary Methods

This section gives the complete analytical procedures, parameters, software versions and random seeds; the Materials and methods section of the main article summarizes them. Numbered citations refer to the reference list of the main article, and Table A–M refer to the tables in S2 File.

### Co-expression networks and module preservation

For GSE76925 [30] and GSE33000 [31] we used the log-scale expression matrices supplied by the submitters, applied quantile normalization and removed outlying samples with standardized sample connectivity z < −2.5; in the brain we also removed age outliers within the AD and control groups by the 1.5 × interquartile range rule, leaving 147 lung and 459 brain participants. Network genes were limited to genes measurable in both the discovery cohort and the corresponding replication cohort, and the 30% with the lowest median absolute deviation were removed. Signed networks were built with WGCNA v1.74 [32] using soft-threshold powers of β = 8 for the lung and 12 for the brain, a minimum module size of 30, deepSplit = 2 and a module merge height of 0.25. Module eigengene–trait relationships were described by Pearson correlation. COPD and AD status were coded as case = 1 and control = 0 and sex as male = 1 and female = 0; GOLD stage was set to 0 when FEV₁/FVC ≥ 0.70 and otherwise to 1–4 by FEV₁ % predicted; emphysema burden on imaging was represented by the percentage of lung area with attenuation below −950 Hounsfield units (LAA-950) and by the lung density at the 15th percentile of the attenuation histogram (Perc15), with higher LAA-950 and lower Perc15 indicating greater burden; and Pi10 is the standardized airway wall thickness. Covariate-adjusted case–control models adjusted for age, sex and smoking pack-years in the lung and for age and sex in the brain, with BH correction within each organ. When the seven age outliers were restored (466 participants in total), with module eigengene loadings fixed and age adjusted for, the disease effects of the brain blue and turquoise modules and their ranks among the 16 non-grey modules were unchanged (Table A in S2 File).

Module preservation was assessed with modulePreservation [33] in GSE47460 (GPL14550 platform) [34] and GSE84422 [35] with 1,000 permutations, reporting Zsummary and medianRank.

### Cross-disease module overlap and functional annotation

Features of both networks were mapped to current NCBI Entrez Gene IDs, and only features that mapped unambiguously to a single Entrez Gene ID were kept. One-sided Fisher's exact tests were applied to the 128 module pairs against a background of the 3,623 non-grey module genes shared by both networks, with BH correction; a sensitivity analysis repeated the tests against the 4,832 genes passing filters in both networks, including grey-module genes. Among module pairs associated positively with disease in both networks, and among pairs associated negatively in both networks, the pair with the lowest overlap FDR was selected, and their shared genes were defined as Axis A and Axis B; all later analyses used these two fixed gene sets (Table B in S2 File). Functional enrichment was assessed by one-sided over-representation analysis of GO biological processes (org.Hs.eg.db and GO.db v3.22.0), KEGG (retrieved with KEGGREST v1.50.0 on 25 June 2026) and MSigDB Hallmark 2026.1.Hs [36] against the 3,623-gene background, with BH correction within each axis and database. The BH-adjusted P value of enrichment tests is denoted q.

### Replication in independent cohorts

The Axis A score of each sample was computed with singscore [37] (v1.30.0) as the mean within-sample expression rank of Axis A members. After standardization of the score, case–control differences in SD units were estimated by linear regression adjusted for age, sex and other covariates available in each dataset, such as smoking status, sequencing plate, post-mortem interval (PMI) and RNA integrity number (RIN) (Table D in S2 File); this model is referred to as the disease-association model of Axis A. Samples with missing covariates were excluded from the corresponding model, and the numbers of samples analyzed in each dataset are given in Table D in S2 File. In the lung, COPD was compared with controls in GSE47460 with adjustment for age, sex and smoking status. The two GSE47460 platforms, GPL14550 and GPL6480, profiled different donors and were analyzed separately without pooling; the other analyses that use GSE47460, including module preservation, cell proportion adjustment, myeloid signatures and member effects, used the GPL14550 platform, which has more donors. Because GSE84422, used for brain preservation, contains only 16 controls, we searched GEO on 3 October 2026 through E-utilities (db = gds) with the query alzheimer AND (frontal OR prefrontal OR BA9 OR BA10 OR BA46) AND Homo sapiens AND gse AND (expression profiling by array OR by high throughput sequencing), which returned 144 records. After removal of single-cell, sorted-cell, microdissected and non-AD records, datasets were selected with prespecified criteria, namely frontal cortex, whole-tissue transcriptome, AD and controls defined by diagnosis, per-sample age and sex, donors independent of the discovery cohort, at least 15 per group and at least 80% of Axis A members measurable. The six eligible datasets were GSE84422, GSE118553 [38], GSE313307 [39], [GSE140841](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE140841), GSE48350 [40] and GSE36980 [41]. GSE44770 was excluded because 227 of its 230 arrays are shared with the discovery cohort GSE33000. In GSE84422, cases classified neuropathologically as possible, probable or definite AD in the original study were combined as AD, and GSE313307 was additionally adjusted for sequencing plate and terminal respiratory infection. In GSE48350, controls were restricted to donors no younger than the youngest AD case. The raw CEL file of GSM1176257, labeled AD, was byte-identical to that of GSM350078 in GSE11882, labeled as a control, and its sex-gene expression matched a male control, so it was removed. Effects were estimated in each dataset and pooled with a DerSimonian–Laird random-effects model [42] (metafor v5.0.1 [43]); sensitivity analyses comprised REML estimation with Hartung–Knapp inference [44], leaving out one dataset at a time, restricting cases to probable or definite AD, restricting to donors without terminal infection, and restricting to members measurable both on the GPL14550 platform of GSE47460 and in the six AD datasets (Table E in S2 File). Duplicate donors across datasets were screened with doppelgangR v1.38.0 [45], and sex labels were checked against expression of Y-linked genes and *XIST* (Table D in S2 File). At the member level, disease effects were estimated with the standardized expression of each member as the outcome in the same model as the score, and the six AD datasets were pooled with the same random-effects model; score effects were recomputed after leaving out each member in turn (Table E in S2 File). Axis B was tested with the same workflow (Fig B in S1 File).

Co-expression of Axis A members in independent datasets was assessed with sigQC v0.1.24 [46] in the expression matrices of both GSE47460 platforms (GPL14550 and GPL6480) and the six AD datasets above, processed as in the replication analysis and without disease labels. The primary metric was the median of the pairwise Spearman correlation matrix of Axis A members, compared with 1,000 random gene sets matched on mean expression (20 bins), with empirical P = (1 + number of random values ≥ observed value) / 1,001 (Table C in S2 File).

### Single-cell localization and donor-level inference

The lung single-cell dataset GSE302339 [14] comprises 65 10x Genomics libraries from 32 COPD and 23 control donors and was analyzed from the filtered count matrices deposited in GEO. The GEO subject IDs cood8 and copd8 have identical clinical records and the laboratory IDs COPD 8 LUL and COPD8 LLL; they are libraries from two lobes of one donor and were analyzed as one donor. Quality control was performed within each library. Cells deviating by more than 3 median absolute deviations from the library median were removed with quickPerCellQC in scuttle v1.20.0 [47], testing total counts and detected genes on the log scale for low outliers and the mitochondrial percentage for high outliers, and doublets were then identified and removed within each library with scDblFinder v1.24.10 [48], leaving 211,464 cells (Table F in S2 File). Post-QC median mitochondrial percentages were also compared across libraries; two libraries exceeded the median across libraries by more than 3 median absolute deviations on the log scale and were removed in a sensitivity analysis. After normalization to 10,000 counts per cell and log1p transformation, each cell was annotated with CellTypist v1.7.1 [49], using its Human_Lung_Atlas model trained on the Human Lung Cell Atlas [50], in best-match mode without majority voting, and the 60 resulting labels were grouped into six broad classes, namely myeloid, T/NK, B/plasma, epithelial, endothelial and stromal cells, with 14 hematopoietic stem cells kept apart (Table F in S2 File). The UMAP embedding was rebuilt in scanpy v1.12.4 following the preprocessing in the original authors' public code, with library effects corrected by harmonypy v2.0.2, the Python implementation of Harmony [51], and used only for display. For the brain we used the SEA-AD single-nucleus data of the DLPFC released on 22 June 2026 [13,52], keeping the published Subclass and Supertype annotations without re-clustering or re-annotation. Axis A scores were computed with UCell v2.14.0 [53] (maxRank = 1500) and aggregated by donor × cell class, taking the donor mean for localization and the donor median for disease association tests; each combination contained at least 20 cells or nuclei. Localization between cell classes was compared with donors as independent units, using fixed-effects models with donor-clustered robust variance (CR2) [54], computed with clubSandwich v0.7.0, and 5,000 expression-matched random gene sets as the reference.

Disease associations were tested at the donor level. In the lung, COPD was compared with controls in myeloid cells and in alveolar macrophages, defined as cells with the CellTypist label Alveolar macrophages, by Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with the Hodges–Lehmann location shift and its 95% CI. In the brain, disease association analyses used only nuclei that were profiled with the 10Xv3.1 method, came from experimental components not flagged as failed and were flagged for inclusion in analysis; in immune supertypes with at least 30 eligible donors, linear models with the standardized local continuous pseudoprogression score of the DLPFC published by SEA-AD (CPS_Local) as the exposure were adjusted for age, sex, PMI, RIN and study batch, with HC3 robust standard errors estimated in statsmodels v0.14.6 and BH correction within each organ (Table F in S2 File). For pseudobulk analyses, raw UMI counts were summed within each donor and cell class (lung myeloid cells, 116,558 cells from 55 donors; lung alveolar macrophages, 64,542 cells from 55 donors; brain Microglia 2, 23,198 nuclei from 79 donors; all brain microglial supertypes, 79 donors) and processed with edgeR v4.8.2 [55] by filterByExpr filtering, trimmed mean of M values (TMM) normalization and dispersion estimation, and tested with fry [56] as a self-contained directional test; the same object was transformed with voom and tested with CAMERA [57] (inter.gene.cor = NA) as a competitive test. Lung models were adjusted for age, sex and smoking status; brain models used the standardized CPS_Local as the primary exposure and SEA-AD AD neuropathologic change (intermediate or high vs not or low) as a secondary exposure, both adjusted for age, sex, PMI, RIN and study batch. A sensitivity analysis raised the minimum number of cells per donor to 50. In the lung, we also tested the difference between the two COPD groups directly in a three-group model of controls, emphysematous COPD and non-emphysematous COPD following the original authors' classification, and used FEV₁ % predicted as a continuous exposure (Table F in S2 File).

### Adjustment for cell proportions

Relative cell proportions in tissue samples were estimated with the CIBERSORTx web tool [58] (October 2026). Single-cell references were sampled evenly by cell type across donors. The lung reference used six major cell classes of GSE302339, namely myeloid, T/NK, B/plasma, epithelial, endothelial and stromal, with 300 cells per class from 55 donors; the brain reference used eight major classes of the SEA-AD DLPFC, namely excitatory neurons, inhibitory neurons, astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, oligodendrocyte precursor cells, immune cells, endothelial cells, and vascular leptomeningeal and perivascular cells, with 250 nuclei per class from 80 donors. In the primary analysis all 137 Axis A genes were removed before the reference was built; a version keeping all genes served as a sensitivity analysis. Signature matrices were built with the default web parameters; proportions were estimated in relative mode with S-mode batch correction, with quantile normalization for microarray data but not for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, and 100 permutations. Log2-scale microarray expression was back-transformed to the linear scale, and RNA-seq data were supplied as counts per million. GSE33000 contains two-color microarray ratios that are unsuitable for CIBERSORTx and was not included. The standardized target cell proportion, myeloid cells in the lung and immune cells in the brain, was added to the disease-association model in the independent datasets and to the same model adjusted for age, sex and smoking pack-years in the lung discovery cohort GSE76925, and disease effects before and after adjustment were compared; the six AD datasets were estimated separately and pooled with the DerSimonian–Laird model. The relationship between the target cell proportion and the Axis A score was described by Pearson correlation, and the case–control difference in the standardized proportion was estimated by linear regression with the covariates of the disease-association model. A sensitivity analysis added all cell proportions except the reference class. In the lung discovery cohort, the myeloid proportion was also added in the same way to the covariate-adjusted model of the red module eigengene. Cell proportions were also estimated with dtangle v2.0.10 [59] in the lung discovery cohort GSE76925 and, as a microglial surrogate proportion, with BRETIGEA v1.0.3 [60] in the brain discovery cohort GSE33000, where it was added to the covariate-adjusted model of the blue module eigengene (Table G in S2 File).

### Comparison with myeloid signatures

Myeloid expression specificity was computed from the CIBERSORTx single-cell reference as the mean counts per million of each gene in the target class divided by the sum of its class means, with myeloid cells as the target class in the lung and immune cells in the brain. In each independent dataset, non-Axis A genes were grouped by mean expression within that dataset (10 quantile bins) and by myeloid specificity in the corresponding organ (5 quantile bins), and 1,000 random gene sets were drawn according to the number of measurable Axis A members in each group; their disease effects were estimated with the same model as for Axis A, and the AD datasets were pooled within each draw with the DerSimonian–Laird model. Empirical P values were one-sided, given as the proportion of random-set effects not smaller than the Axis A effect. The myeloid signatures and functional gene sets were the top 250 BRETIGEA human microglial marker genes [60], the human core microglial signature of Patir et al. [61], an adult lung macrophage signature [15] (MSigDB C8), and the GO gene sets complement activation (GO:0006956) and phagocytosis (GO:0006909). For each signature we report member overlap with Axis A, score correlation, the disease effect of the signature itself, and the disease effect of Axis A with the signature score added to the model together with the variance inflation factor; the analysis was repeated after removing shared members (Table I in S2 File).

### Genetic analyses

MAGMA v1.10 [62] (NCBI build 37.3 gene coordinates, 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European linkage disequilibrium reference, SNP-wise mean model) was used to test one-sided competitive enrichment of the two axes in AD [63] and COPD [64] GWAS summary statistics, with Holm correction across the four tests. Default settings were used, with one-sided positive tests conditioned on gene size, gene density, inverse minor allele count and their logarithms; because per-SNP sample sizes vary in the AD summary statistics, per-gene sample size and its logarithm were also conditioned on by default. The COPD GWAS phenotype was a COPD liability score inferred by a deep learning model from raw spirograms. In sensitivity analyses, the MAGMA gene-exclude option removed all genes within 1 Mb of *APOE* (NCBI build 37.3 coordinates) before the AD test was repeated, and the gene-include option restricted the background to the 4,832 genes passing filters in both networks before the four tests were repeated. In conditional analyses, the top 250 BRETIGEA human microglial marker genes (all 1,000 in a sensitivity analysis) and the Patir core signature were each entered as gene sets and used as covariates with the MAGMA condition option before Axis A and Axis B were tested again (Table J in S2 File). Variants were matched to the reference panel by rsID with the MAGMA synonym file, and SNPs were assigned to genes within gene boundaries without flanking windows. The AD summary statistics contributed 13,648,198 SNPs and 18,224 genes, with per-variant sample sizes of up to 147,103, and the COPD summary statistics 11,287,261 SNPs and 18,268 genes from 325,027 participants. Of the 137 Axis A genes, 130 were tested; five lie on chromosome X and two lack build 37.3 gene locations. Of the 434 Axis B genes, 409 (AD) and 410 (COPD) were tested. As prespecified sensitivity analyses, the AD tests were repeated with gene windows of ±10 kb and of 35 kb upstream and 10 kb downstream, with Holm correction across the two axes within each window (Table J in S2 File).

### Peripheral blood analyses

Peripheral blood analyses included six datasets with defined cases and controls and per-sample age and sex, namely GSE54837 [65], GSE248493 [66] and E-MTAB-5278 [67] for COPD and GSE63060, GSE63061 [68] and GSE248417 [69] for AD; GSE248493 consists of PBMC samples and the others of whole blood. In GSE63060 and GSE63061, AD and control samples flagged by the submitters as included in the AddNeuroMed case–control study were used. Among the microarray datasets, GSE54837 was log2-transformed and the others used the log2-scale matrices supplied by the submitters, all summarized to Entrez Gene; RNA-seq data underwent low-expression filtering, edgeR TMM normalization and voom transformation. In each dataset, limma v3.66.0 [70] fitted a case–control model adjusted for age and sex; Axis A was tested with CAMERA [57] (inter.gene.cor = NA) with BH correction across the six datasets as one family; the Axis A score of each participant was computed with singscore, standardized and used to estimate the case–control difference with 95% CI from the same design matrix, reported per dataset without pooling. Axis A was also tested with fgseaMultilevel in fgsea v1.36.2 [71] on genes ranked by moderated t statistics. Duplicate donors between GSE63060 and GSE63061 were checked by between-sample correlation of gene-centered expression together with age and sex (Table K in S2 File).

### Disease scope

Disease scope analyses used the disease-association model of Axis A to compare other diseases with their controls in public datasets of matching tissue, including chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis and IPF in lung tissue and ever- versus never-smokers without lung disease (GSE150910 [72]), and Huntington's disease (GSE64810 [73]), Parkinson's disease (GSE68719 [74]), and schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and major depression (GSE53987 [75], Brodmann area 46 [BA46]) in frontal cortex. Comparator diseases were BH-corrected within prespecified lung and brain comparison families. In GSE150910, all chronic hypersensitivity pneumonitis samples were sequenced on ten processing dates in June–July 2020, whereas all controls were sequenced on four processing dates in December or in other batches; because disease and processing batch were completely confounded, this comparison was excluded from BH correction in the lung family and is listed descriptively in Table D in S2 File only; the lung family comprised the IPF and ever-smoker comparisons. Samples, covariates and data processing for each dataset are given in Table D in S2 File. On both GSE47460 platforms, IPF/UIP and other interstitial lung diseases were also compared with controls, and COPD with IPF/UIP, using the same model. These prespecified secondary comparisons are descriptive and are outside the BH families (Table D (e) in S2 File).

### Regulatory networks, virtual knockout and transcription factor activity

Modeled cell populations were chosen by ranking donor-mean Axis A scores and by donor coverage. Alveolar macrophages ranked second among lung myeloid subtypes and covered all 55 donors; Microglia 2 ranked first among brain immune supertypes. Regulatory networks were built with CellOracle v0.20.0 [12] for alveolar macrophages (a fixed sample of 30,000 of 64,542 cells from 55 donors) and Microglia 2 (all 25,056 DLPFC nuclei from 80 donors), with patient and control cells combined. Networks were based on the hg38 promoter prior provided by CellOracle and included the top 3,000 highly variable genes, measurable Axis A members and detected transcription factors from the HumanTFs v1.01 catalogue [76], fitted with bagging ridge regression. *FER1L4* had no unique stable feature in either model, leaving 136 measurable members. Expression was normalized per cell and then natural-log transformed and imputed by k-nearest neighbors in CellOracle, and delta_X is the difference between simulated and imputed values on this scale. Each of the 357 transcription factors testable in both models was set to zero in turn, the expression change after three rounds of propagation was simulated and averaged with equal weight across donors; genes predicted to decrease in both models were counted as shared decreases, genes predicted to increase in both models as shared increases, and other non-zero changes as opposite-direction changes. Network out-degree is the number of target genes with non-zero coefficients for a transcription factor (Table L in S2 File).

Transcription factor activities were inferred with the univariate linear model of decoupleR v2.16.0 [77] (ulm, minsize = 5) on log counts per million of donor pseudobulk, with CollecTRI [78] as the regulatory prior (retrieved from OmniPath on 19 October 2026, processed as in decoupleR get_collectri, with complexes merged into AP1 and NFKB). Activities were tested with limma using the design matrices of the pseudobulk tests; the ten top-ranked CellOracle transcription factors formed the primary test family and all transcription factors a secondary family, each with BH correction. Pearson correlations of activities with the donor Axis A score are also reported, and the analysis was repeated after removing Axis A genes from the regulatory prior (Table M in S2 File).

### Statistical analysis

The independent statistical unit was the participant or donor; in single-cell analyses, cells or nuclei from the same donor were not treated as independent biological replicates, and all disease-related tests were performed at the donor level. The Fisher tests of module overlap, the over-representation analyses of functional enrichment, the MAGMA competitive tests and the random gene set references in the co-expression, localization and myeloid reference analyses were one-sided, and all other tests were two-sided. All steps involving random sampling used fixed random seeds, including the expression-matched random gene sets of single-cell localization (lung 20260901, brain 20260831), the sigQC random gene sets (20261014), the random gene sets matched for both expression and myeloid specificity (20261019), and cell sampling and network fitting in CellOracle (123). Multiple testing was corrected within the test families described in each section, using the BH method to control the FDR or the Holm method to control the family-wise error rate; statistical significance was defined as BH-adjusted FDR < 0.05 or Holm-adjusted P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed in R v4.5.3, except the donor-level pathology association tests in the brain, which were performed in Python v3.9.6; single-cell annotation and CellOracle analyses were performed in Python v3.9.6, and the lung UMAP embedding was rebuilt in Python v3.12.14.

The choice of each test follows the original publication or official implementation of the method. (1) Module overlap and functional enrichment. The official WGCNA function overlapTable evaluates gene overlap between two sets of modules with a one-sided Fisher's exact test, the upper tail of the hypergeometric distribution [32]; the module overlap tests in this study follow this approach, and the over-representation analyses of functional enrichment use the same test. (2) Replication in independent cohorts and disease scope. The original singscore paper states that its scores are computed for each sample independently of other samples and therefore do not change with sample composition or sample size [37]; accordingly, this study computed the same Axis A score separately in datasets from different platforms and compared cases with controls by covariate-adjusted linear regression. (3) Pooling across datasets. The original DerSimonian–Laird paper proposed a random-effects method for combining studies that allows effect sizes to differ between studies [42]; the six AD datasets differ in platform, brain region and diagnostic approach, so this method was used, and Hartung–Knapp inference, which builds confidence intervals from the t distribution with a refined variance estimate when few studies are pooled [44], served as a sensitivity analysis; in this study its variance scaling factor was 1.01, so the modified Hartung–Knapp method recommended by Röver et al. [44] gave the same interval (Table E in S2 File). (4) Gene set tests. The original CAMERA paper shows that inter-gene correlation within a set makes competitive tests that ignore it anti-conservative, and CAMERA estimates and corrects for this correlation [57]; the rotation test proposed in the original ROAST paper allows inter-gene correlation and applies to any design that can be written as a linear model [56], and fry is its fast approximation in limma and edgeR; the edgeR v4 paper analyzes count data with negative binomial generalized linear models [55]; and the original limma paper describes how borrowing information across genes overcomes small sample sizes [70]. (5) Genetic enrichment. The original MAGMA paper defines competitive gene set analysis as testing whether genes in a set are more strongly associated than other genes, and strongly recommends correcting for confounders such as gene size and gene density [62]. (6) Single-cell localization. The original paper of Pustejovsky and Tipton notes that fixed-effects models are often combined with cluster-robust variance, and that with few clusters the CR2 correction with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom keeps the type I error close to the nominal level [54]. Because each donor contributes scores for several cell classes in this study, this method was used with donors as clusters. (7) Adjustment for cell proportions. The original CIBERSORTx paper builds signature matrices from single-cell references and uses S-mode to correct platform differences between single-cell references and bulk data [58]; the original dtangle paper notes that estimated cell proportions are often used as covariates in differential expression analysis [59]. (8) Gene set co-expression. The original sigQC paper evaluates the expression, variability and structure of existing gene signatures in other datasets, including the tightness of correlation among members [46]. (9) Transcription factor activity. The original decoupleR paper compared many activity inference methods and recommends ulm among them [77]; the original CollecTRI paper shows that activities inferred with its regulatory prior are more accurate than those from earlier priors [78].
